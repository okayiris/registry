# EU consumer rights

## What it is

Rules that protect people buying from a business anywhere in the EU. They are EU law, so they hold in every
member country; national law can add more, never less. This skill covers the parts an assistant meets most:
returns, guarantees and help. It does **not** cover buying from a private person: these rules only apply
when the seller is a business.

## The 14-day right of withdrawal

When the owner buys **online, by phone or at the door** (a distance or off-premises contract), they can
cancel within **14 days without giving a reason**:

- for goods, 14 days from delivery; for services, 14 days from the day the contract was made;
- a deadline on a non-working day moves to the next working day;
- some countries give 30 days after unsolicited doorstep selling.

**Return costs:** the owner pays postage for a withdrawal, unless the seller offered to pay or did not say
beforehand that the buyer pays. A **defective** product is different: then the seller pays the return.

**No right of withdrawal** for, among others:

- tickets, hotel bookings, car rental and catering for specific dates;
- food and other goods that spoil quickly;
- made-to-order or clearly personalised goods;
- sealed audio, video or software once unsealed;
- digital content (a download or stream) once started after agreeing to lose the right;
- services fully performed after the owner agreed to start at once and lose the right;
- urgent repairs, such as a plumber for a leak.

## The 2-year legal guarantee

If goods are faulty, or do not look or work as advertised:

- the seller must **repair or replace** them at no cost. If that is impossible, or takes too long or causes
  significant inconvenience, the owner is entitled to a full or partial refund;
- the guarantee lasts **at least 2 years from receiving the goods**. Some countries give longer;
- if a defect shows within **1 year**, the seller has to prove it was not there at delivery (in some
  countries 2 years);
- a shop's or maker's own "warranty" (commercial guarantee) comes **on top** of this and can never reduce it.
  "Your 6-month warranty has run out" does not end the 2-year legal guarantee;
- second-hand goods from a business are covered too; some countries allow agreeing a shorter period, but
  never under 1 year;
- **digital content and services** (apps, games, streaming, cloud storage) have a legal guarantee too, also
  when paid for with personal data instead of money.

## How the assistant helps

1. Establish the facts: bought from a business or a person? Online, at the door, or in a shop? When
   delivered? What is wrong?
2. Say which right applies, and the deadline, with the date worked out.
3. Draft the message to the seller: what was bought, when, what the problem is, which right the owner uses,
   and what they ask (return and refund, or repair). The owner approves and sends it (see
   `acting-on-behalf`).
4. Keep the proof: order confirmation, delivery date, photos of the defect, the correspondence.
5. If the seller refuses and is in **another EU country**, the **European Consumer Centre** in the owner's
   country can help. For a seller in the same country, the country pages on Your Europe lead to the
   national rules and where to turn.

## What not to claim

- National rules can be more generous; check the owner's country on Your Europe before saying a right ends.
- Do not promise an outcome. The seller may dispute whether a defect was caused by use.
- For a seller outside the EU, these EU rules may not apply; say so.

## Where this stops

This follows the EU's official guidance on Your Europe for returns, guarantees and consumer rights when
shopping. It is not legal advice; for a dispute with real money at stake, point to the European Consumer
Centre or a lawyer.
