# Building habits

## What it is

A habit, in psychology, is an action triggered automatically by a situation it has been linked to, like
putting on a seatbelt after getting into the car. Repeating a simple action in the same situation builds
that link, until the action happens without thinking about it (Gardner, Lally and Wardle, 2012).

This matters because motivation fades. Advice that relies on keeping motivation up tends to work only
briefly. A habit keeps going when motivation drops.

## The method

This follows the tool the same authors wrote for patients in general practice:

1. **Pick a goal** the owner wants: "meer bewegen", "minder op mijn telefoon", "elke dag Spaans".
2. **Choose one simple action** towards it that can be done every day. Smaller is better: simple actions,
   like drinking a glass of water, became automatic faster than elaborate ones, like fifty sit-ups.
3. **Tie it to a fixed moment and place** the owner meets every day: "na het ontbijt", "als ik thuiskom
   van werk", "na het tandenpoetsen 's avonds". Write it as a plan: "Na [moment] ga ik [actie]."
4. **Do it every time that moment comes.**
5. **It gets easier.** In the study behind this advice, how automatic the action felt rose quickly at first
   and levelled off after **66 days on average**. The authors tell patients to expect it within about ten
   weeks.

Three details from the same source:

- **The owner chooses the action.** A change picked for its personal value is an easier habit than one
  picked to satisfy someone else.
- **Keep it the same.** Varying the action or the moment to stay interested costs effort and gets in the way
  of it becoming automatic.
- **Pick something to do, not something to stop.** It is not possible to form a habit of *not* doing
  something. "Na het eten een stuk wandelen" works; "niet meer snoepen" does not.

## How the assistant helps

- **Help choose the smallest version** that still counts. When the owner wants to run 5 km daily, suggest
  starting with putting on running shoes and walking ten minutes after breakfast.
- **Help find the moment.** Look at the owner's actual routine (see `planning-a-day`) for something that
  happens every day at the same place.
- **Offer a simple tracker**, if the owner wants one: a tick per day, and once a week the question "hoe
  automatisch voelt het nu, van 1 tot 10?". The authors suggest exactly this, to see it getting easier.
- **One reminder at the chosen moment**, only in the first weeks, only if asked. The goal is that the
  moment itself becomes the reminder, not the assistant.

## When a day is missed

**One missed day does not ruin it.** In the study, missing an occasional opportunity did not seriously
harm habit formation: progress picked up again after the miss. Say so plainly: "Gisteren niet gelukt, dat
maakt niet uit. Vandaag gewoon weer na het ontbijt."

Never count misses back to the owner, and never make a streak the goal (see `encouragement`).

## Stopping a habit

Breaking an existing habit needs different techniques than forming a new one, and this skill does not
cover them. What it can do: help the owner choose a new action for the same moment ("als ik op de bank ga
zitten, leg ik mijn telefoon in de gang"). For addiction (smoking, alcohol, drugs, gambling), point to the
GP; the Dutch GP guidance names the GP as the way to further help for alcohol and drugs.

## What not to do

- Not five new habits at once. One, until it runs by itself.
- No rewards or point systems for something the owner wants for its own sake (see `encouragement`).
- Do not decide for the owner which habits are good for them.

## Where this stops

This uses Gardner, Lally and Wardle's review of habit formation for general practice, which includes their
patient tool and summarises Lally's study of how long habits take to form. It is for everyday habits, not
for treating addiction or compulsive behaviour.
