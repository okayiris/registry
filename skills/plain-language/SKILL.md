# Plain language

## What it is

A text is in plain language when the people it is for can easily find what they need, understand what
they find, and use it. That is the definition plain language experts adopted in 2014, and the basis of the
ISO standard for plain language. It is about the reader, not about a word count: the words, the order, the
headings and the layout all count.

This skill covers two traditions that say much the same thing:

- **Dutch at level B1** (taalniveau B1), as the Dutch central government writes for Rijksoverheid.nl;
- **English plain language**, from the US federal plain language guides (now on digital.gov) and the
  ISO 24495-1 standard.

## Who it is for

**Plain language is not dumbing down.** The US guide calls that one of the most common myths: write for
your actual readers, with their knowledge and interests. Do not write for a school class unless your
readers are one.

The Dutch government says the same from the other side:

- there are six language levels, from A1 (lowest) to C2 (highest). Governments and companies mostly write at
  C1, which many people do not understand well;
- B1 is simple Dutch. The large majority of people understand it, including people without (higher)
  education;
- higher educated people and professionals usually also **prefer** B1 over C1: it reads easier and faster,
  especially on a phone;
- for about 20% of the Dutch population B1 is still too hard, among them 2.5 million people with low
  literacy. For them, A2 or pictures and a short video help. Research for the government found that good
  readers read A2 texts just as easily and felt taken seriously by them.

## The ISO standard's four principles

ISO 24495-1:2023 (Plain language, Part 1: Governing principles and guidelines) sets four principles:

1. **Relevant:** readers get what they need.
2. **Findable:** readers can easily find what they need.
3. **Understandable:** readers can easily understand what they find.
4. **Usable:** readers can easily use the information.

The standard is language neutral, drafted by experts from 25 countries. Only a small part of it is about
word choice; most is about content, structure and design. Countries may add their own practices, such as
sentence length.

## How to write it

### Start with the reader

- Who reads this, and what do they need to know or do? Tell only that. Leave out what they do not need now.
- Put the most important thing first. A letter says what the reader must do, and by when, at the top.

### Words

- Use common words that almost everyone uses. Explain a term you cannot avoid, in the text itself.
- No jargon, no figures of speech. "Ze is heel erg boos", not "Ze kookt van woede".
- In Dutch, prefer everyday linking words: "maar, want, als", not "daarentegen, immers, indien".

| Instead of (C1) | Write (B1) |
|---|---|
| betreffende | over |
| creëren | maken, vormen |
| prioriteit | voorrang, voorkeur |
| relevant | belangrijk |
| verstrekken | geven |

- In English, avoid **hidden verbs**: a verb turned into a noun, often ending in -ment, -tion, -sion or
  -ance. "We analyze data", not "We conduct an analysis of the data".
- Names of laws and legal terms can stay, but explain them.

### Sentences

- **Short sentences, one idea each.** A long sentence full of exceptions loses the main point. Split it.
- **Active voice.** It says who does what. "You must include this information", not "This information must
  be included". Passive voice often hides who acts. It is acceptable when there really is no actor except
  the law.
- **Present tense** where you can. It is simpler and more direct than conditional or future forms.
- In English, a passive sentence usually has a form of "to be" (is, was, were, have been) plus a past
  participle (often ending in -ed). That is a quick way to find them.

### Structure

- A clear title and **headings that say what follows**. The Dutch government's inclusive language tips
  suggest short headings of at most three words.
- Group what belongs together, and put it in a logical order.
- **Short paragraphs, one topic each**, starting with a sentence that sums it up. The US guide cites
  writing experts: no more than 150 words and three to eight sentences, and never over 250 words.
- Use **lists** for steps and options, and **tables** to make complex relationships clear.
- Emphasise with bold, sparingly. Avoid underlining (it looks like a link) and full capitals (harder to
  read, and online it reads as shouting). The Dutch tips also advise against italics.
- Use a clear, large enough font.

### Tone

- Simple is not childish. The Dutch tips say it outright: no condescending or childish language.
- Give an example where it helps: the Dutch B1 guidance names an active style with examples.

## Test it with readers

A text is plain when readers understand it, not when a checker says so. The US guide says to test early,
before a text is finished, then fix and test again, at least twice.

**Paraphrase test** (good for short texts, letters, pages and survey questions):

1. Find 6 to 9 people like the real readers.
2. Let each read one section, then ask: "What does this mean, in your own words?" Write down their words.
   Do not correct them.
3. Ask open questions: "What would you do after reading this?" and "What might confuse other people?"
   People admit more easily what others might find hard.
4. Avoid yes or no questions. "Is it clear?" gets "yes", even when it is not.
5. Wherever someone misunderstood, the text has a problem. Fix it and test again.

In the US guide's examples, readers said a letter was clear yet would have called or acted wrongly, because
"shortly" had no date and a term meant different things to different readers. Understanding every word is
not the same as knowing what to do.

For Dutch texts, the Dutch government has test panels with former low-literate readers through Stichting ABC.

For the owner, a quick version: ask one person who is not in their field to read it and say back what it
asks of them.

## How the assistant works with it

1. Ask who the readers are and what they must do after reading (see `asking-good-questions`).
2. Rewrite: the point first, then short sentences, common words, active voice, headings, lists.
3. Show the owner the new version next to the old one, with a line on what changed.
4. Keep facts, amounts, dates and legal terms exactly right. Simpler words must not change the meaning.
5. Suggest a paraphrase test with one or two real readers for anything that goes to many people.

## What not to claim

- Do not say a text "is B1" because it has short sentences. Level is judged on the whole text; the
  government uses special software and a yearly scan for Rijksoverheid.nl, and even that measures, it does
  not replace readers.
- Do not promise that a sentence length rule makes a text clear. Structure and content matter more.
- Do not simplify a legal or medical text so far that it says something different. Explain the term instead.

## Where this stops

This follows CommunicatieRijk (the Dutch central government's communication guidance) on taalniveau B1,
understandable communication and inclusive language, the US federal plain language guides on digital.gov
(which carried forward PlainLanguage.gov), and the International Plain Language Federation's pages on the
ISO 24495 standard and the plain language definition. The ISO standard itself is sold by ISO and was not
read in full here. For easy-read (A2 or lower) or sign language versions, work with the specialists these
guides name.
