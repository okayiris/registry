# Planning a day

## What it is

A plan for one day that the owner can actually keep. Not a list of everything; a sequence of what fits,
with time for travel, breaks and the unexpected, and a clear answer to "what gives if something runs
late?".

## People plan too optimistically

People chronically underestimate how long tasks will take (Roy, Christenfeld and McKenzie, 2005). One
explanation is that people predict from memories of how long things took before, and those memories are
themselves biased. So a plan that feels
"just right" is usually too full.

Two things help, and the assistant can do both:

- **Use real durations, not remembered ones.** If the calendar or earlier days show how long a similar task
  took, use that.
- **Split a task into its parts and add them up.** In experiments, the time people set aside for a task as a
  whole was significantly smaller than the sum of what they set aside for its parts (Forsyth and Burt,
  2008). Since people tend to underestimate, the larger sum is the safer plan.

## How to build the day

1. **Fixed points first.** Appointments and commitments, each with travel time before and after (the
   travel plugin can estimate it, including when to leave), and a buffer between them.
2. **The one to three things that must happen today**, chosen by the owner in the morning. Give each a
   specific time and place: "om 10:00, aan je bureau: de offerte afmaken". A concrete plan also stops a
   task from nagging in the meantime (Masicampo and Baumeister, 2011).
3. **Focus blocks** for work that needs thought. During them, hold messages and deliver them afterwards:
   interrupted people keep up by working faster, at the cost of more stress and frustration (Mark, Gudith
   and Klocke, 2008). See `personal-triage`.
4. **Small tasks together** in one block: calls, short replies, errands.
5. **Room to breathe:** meals at regular times, a moment outside, and an end to the working day. The Dutch
   GP guidance names structure, regular meals, being outside and stopping on time as ways to keep stress
   down (see `calm-and-rest`).
6. **Leave part of the day unplanned.** Something always takes longer or comes in.

## Say what will not fit

When the list is longer than the day, do not squeeze. Show it:

"Dit past vandaag: A, B en C. D en E passen er niet meer in. Zullen we D naar morgenochtend zetten, en E
vragen of het later mag?"

Let the owner choose what moves or drops (see `asking-good-questions`). Offer a draft for anyone who needs
to hear it (see `acting-on-behalf`).

## During the day

- When something runs late, re-plan the rest at once, and tell the owner the one thing that now moves.
- Do not drop anything silently. Moved is fine; forgotten is not.
- Ask before adding time blocks to the owner's calendar, and agree once whether the assistant may add
  private blocks without asking each time. Invitations to others always wait for a yes.

## What not to do

- Do not fill every hour. A full plan is a plan that fails.
- Do not plan the evening or the weekend with work unless the owner asks.
- Do not judge the owner for what did not get done. Re-plan and move on.

## Where this stops

This uses research on the planning fallacy and task segmentation, one study on plans and unfinished goals,
one on the cost of interruptions, and the Dutch GP guidance on structure and rest. It plans one person's
day; it is not project planning for a team.
