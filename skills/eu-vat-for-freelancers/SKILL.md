# EU VAT for freelancers

## What it is

VAT rules are set by one EU directive and applied by each country. The cross-border rules below hold in
every EU country; the rates, deadlines and national thresholds differ. For the Dutch details, see
`dutch-vat-zzp`. This skill is for a business **established in the EU**.

## Selling across borders

| Selling | To | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| **Services** | A business in another EU country | Usually no VAT: the customer pays VAT in their own country through the **reverse charge**. |
| **Services** | A consumer in another EU country | Usually VAT at the rate of the owner's own country. **Except telecommunications, broadcasting and electronic services**, which are taxed in the customer's country. |
| **Goods** | A business in another EU country, goods sent there | No VAT, **if the customer has a valid EU VAT number**. Without one, usually the owner's own rate. |
| **Goods** | A consumer in another EU country (distance sales) | Below **EUR 10,000** a year of such sales across the EU, VAT may stay in the owner's country; above it, the customer's country's rate applies. One registration in one country can cover declaring and paying VAT on all these EU sales. |
| **Goods or services** | Customers outside the EU | Usually no VAT (a country may tax a service used there). |

In all cases where the owner does not charge VAT on a sale, they can usually still deduct the VAT they paid
on costs for that sale.

**Always check the customer's VAT number** before leaving VAT off an invoice (the EU's VIES check). And each
row has exceptions: look up the specific case before relying on the general rule.

## Buying across borders

When the owner buys goods or services for the business from another EU country, they usually declare and
pay the VAT themselves, at their own country's rate, as if they had made the sale, and can usually deduct
it in the same return. Services bought from outside the EU work the same way; goods from outside the EU pay
VAT at import.

## The small business exemption (SME scheme)

Every EU country can exempt small businesses from VAT. Under the scheme, the business charges no VAT, but
**cannot deduct VAT on its costs** and does not show VAT on invoices. It can choose normal VAT instead.

- **Domestic:** for a turnover below the country's own threshold, which can be at most **EUR 85,000** a
  year. Each country sets its own (the Netherlands: EUR 20,000, see `dutch-vat-zzp`).
- **Cross-border (since 1 January 2025):** a small business can also use the exemption in **other** EU
  countries if its total EU-wide turnover stays under **EUR 100,000** (this year and last year) and it stays
  under the national threshold of each country where it uses it. It registers **once**, in its home
  country, gets an **EX number** to use in each country, and files **one quarterly report** at home.
  Registration takes up to 35 working days.

## How the assistant helps

1. Ask where the customer is, whether they are a business with a VAT number, and whether it is a service or
   goods.
2. Give the rule from the table, name the exceptions page, and say what goes on the invoice (for reverse
   charge: no VAT, and the customer's VAT number; see `dutch-vat-zzp` for the Dutch invoice wording).
3. For the SME scheme, lay out both sides: no VAT to charge, but no VAT back on costs.
4. For anything unusual (digital services to consumers, events, property, transport), point to the
   country's tax office or an adviser.

## What not to claim

- Rates, thresholds and deadlines per country change; look them up for the country and year.
- The table gives the usual rule. Several exceptions exist; say "usually" and check.
- This is not tax advice for special schemes (margin, travel agents, farmers).

## Where this stops

This follows the EU's official guidance on Your Europe for cross-border VAT and VAT exemptions, and the
Commission's portal on the SME scheme. It covers businesses established in the EU.
