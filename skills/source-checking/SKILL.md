# Source checking

## What it is

Before the assistant repeats a claim, or acts on it, it checks where the claim comes from. Not everything
needs a full check: a restaurant's opening hours do not. A rule, a deadline, a number, a health claim, a quote,
or anything that surprises you does.

The method below comes from research on how professional fact checkers work, and from how libraries teach
the difference between kinds of sources.

## Three questions

The Civic Online Reasoning curriculum (built on observations of professional fact checkers) asks three
questions of anything found online:

1. **Who is behind the information?**
2. **What is the evidence?**
3. **What do other sources say?**

Everything below is a way of answering one of these.

## Primary, secondary, tertiary

| Kind | What it is | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary** | First-hand testimony or direct evidence: the work itself, the actual data or research results, the original document. | The law's text, the tax office's own page, a standard, a scientific article with original findings, statistics from the office that collected them, a letter. |
| **Secondary** | Written by people using the primary sources, to explain or interpret them. | A news article about a study, a blog explaining a law, a book about an event. |
| **Tertiary** | Summaries that digest primary and secondary sources to give background. | Encyclopedias, handbooks, biographical dictionaries. |

What counts as primary depends on the field: in history it is a document from the time, in science the
publication of original research, in the social sciences perhaps survey data.

**How to use this:** find the primary source for anything that matters, and read it yourself. A secondary
source is a good way to find the primary one, and a good way to understand it, but when they disagree, the
primary source wins. An encyclopedia is a starting point, not a citation.

## Read laterally

In a study of 45 experienced internet users (10 historians with a PhD, 10 professional fact checkers and 25
Stanford undergraduates), Wineburg and McGrew found:

- historians and students often fell for features that are easy to fake, such as official-looking logos and
  domain names. They **read vertically**: they stayed on the site to judge it;
- fact checkers **read laterally**: after a quick scan they left the site and opened new tabs to find out
  what others say about it;
- the fact checkers reached better-founded conclusions in a fraction of the time.

Later reviews describe lateral reading as leaving an unfamiliar website to search for information about the
source's credibility elsewhere, and report that explicit teaching of it improved people's evaluations, from
primary school to adults. It is one of the nine kinds of intervention in a 2024 research toolbox against
online misinformation.

**How to do it:**

1. Do not start by reading the page closely. Glance at it: what does it claim, who says it is theirs?
2. Search for the **name of the organisation or author**, not the claim. What do others say about them? Who
   funds them? Is there an encyclopedia entry, a news story, a registry entry?
3. Then search for **the claim itself**. Who else reports it, and where did they get it?
4. Only then decide whether the page is worth reading closely.

A professional look, a `.org` address, a logo or an "about us" page proves nothing: those are exactly the
things the study found easy to fake.

## A second, independent source

For anything surprising, find a second source. The Iris skills registry asks for two independent sources
for anything that surprises you, and it is good practice everywhere.

"Independent" is the hard part:

- two news sites quoting the same press release are **one** source;
- a blog that links to the article you already read is not a second source;
- a second source is independent when it got its information another way: its own data, its own reading of
  the primary source, its own witness.

If you can only find one source for a surprising claim, say so: "Ik vind dit alleen bij deze ene site."

## Dates and versions

A rule, a rate or a figure holds **from** a date, and sometimes **until** one.

- Look for the date on the page: when was it published, when last updated? A page without a date is weaker.
- For a law, ask which version was in force on the date that matters. For Dutch law, the `dutch-laws` server
  reads one article exactly as it was in force on a given date.
- For software, an API or a protocol, note the version the page is about. Documentation for version 2 does
  not tell you how version 3 behaves.
- A figure from last year is a wrong figure for this year until you have checked it. Write the date next to
  every number you keep ("btw-tarief, gecontroleerd 28 september 2026").

## Quotes and where they come from

A quote can end up attached to a famous name it never belonged to. Check before repeating one, and
certainly before the owner puts it in a speech or a post.

An example from Quote Investigator: "Insanity is doing the same thing over and over again and expecting
different results" is usually credited to Albert Einstein. There is no substantive evidence he said it; the
reference work *The Ultimate Quotable Einstein* (Princeton University Press) lists it as misattributed. The
earliest strong match Quote Investigator found is a Knoxville newspaper article from October 1981, where an
attendee of an Al-Anon meeting said it.

**How to check a quote:**

1. Search the exact words in quotation marks, and add "quote origin" or "quote investigator".
2. Look for the **earliest dated appearance** you can find, and who is named there.
3. A quote attributed to a famous person with no source (book, speech, letter, date) is unverified.
4. If it cannot be traced, the owner can still use the words, without the name, or with "often attributed
   to".

## Statistics

Numbers look precise, which makes them persuasive. Research on health statistics by Gigerenzer and
colleagues found that patients, journalists and doctors alike often misread them, partly because numbers are
presented in ways that make benefits look big. Their example: "mammography screening reduces the risk of
dying from breast cancer by 25%" in fact means that 1 fewer woman out of 1,000 dies of the disease.

They recommend, and you can ask for:

| Instead of | Ask for |
|---|---|
| A relative risk ("25% less risk") | The **absolute** risk: out of how many, how many fewer? |
| A single-event probability ("a 30% chance") | A **frequency**: 30 out of 100 people like you |
| Survival rates | **Mortality** rates |
| Conditional probabilities | **Natural frequencies**: out of 1,000 people, how many... |

Also ask of any number: who counted it, how, when, and compared to what? A percentage without its base, or
a rise without the starting point, cannot be judged. Find the office or study that produced the number, and
read it there.

## How the assistant says it

- Say how sure you are and why: "Dit staat op de site van de Belastingdienst zelf, bijgewerkt in 2026."
- Or: "Ik vind dit alleen in één blog, niet bij de bron zelf. Ik zou er niet op bouwen."
- Give the link so the owner can look for themselves.
- Correct gently. The owner forwarding a false claim is not a fool; the claim was made to be believed.

## What not to claim

- Do not say "this is false" when you only could not find a source. Say "I cannot confirm this."
- Do not treat many copies of one claim as many sources.
- Do not cite a secondary source when the primary one is readable.
- Do not state a number, rate, date or rule from memory as current. Read it today, or say it is unchecked.

## Where this stops

This draws on Wineburg and McGrew's study of fact checkers (Teachers College Record, 2019, read through its
ERIC abstract), the Civic Online Reasoning curriculum, research reviews on lateral reading and misinformation
(McGrew 2024; Kozyreva and colleagues 2023 and 2024, abstracts), Cornell University Library's guide to
primary, secondary and tertiary sources, Quote Investigator, and Gigerenzer and colleagues on health
statistics (2007, abstract). It is a method, not a verdict: some claims cannot be settled with what is
public, and then the honest answer is that it is not known.
