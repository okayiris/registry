# Daily and weekly review

## What it is

Three short, regular moments that keep a life in order: a look at the day in the morning, an optional close
at the end of it, and a weekly review. The assistant does the gathering and sorting beforehand; the owner
only looks and decides. The weekly review follows Getting Things Done (GTD), whose author calls it the one
habit the whole method depends on.

## Why closing loops matters

Unfinished goals stay on the mind: in experiments they caused intrusive thoughts and made unrelated tasks
go worse. **Making a specific plan for them removed those effects**, even before the goal itself was done
(Masicampo and Baumeister, 2011). So the point of a review is not only order. Writing down what is still
open, and when it will get attention, lets the owner stop thinking about it.

## The start of the day

Short: a few minutes, one message. Prepare it from the calendar, the triage (see `personal-triage`) and
yesterday's close.

1. **Fixed points:** appointments, with travel time and where the gaps are.
2. **What needs the owner today:** the Today bucket, each with a proposed answer or draft.
3. **One question:** "Wat moet vandaag echt gebeuren?" Offer the two or three likely candidates, and let the
   owner choose (see `asking-good-questions`).

Then help plan the day around that choice (see `planning-a-day`).

## The end of the day (if the owner wants it)

1. What got done: one line, no score.
2. What is still open: for each item, **when** it will get attention next and what the first step is. That
   specific plan is what lets the mind let go.
3. What moves to tomorrow, and anything that can simply be dropped.

After that, the working day is over. No work messages until the next morning unless they are truly urgent
(see `calm-and-rest`).

## The weekly review

David Allen suggests early Friday afternoon, because it surfaces small actions that can still be done while
people are at work. Put it in the calendar for the next four Fridays until it is a habit, and let the owner
shape their own checklist. It has three parts:

### Get clear

- Gather everything loose: receipts, notes, photos of papers, voice memos.
- Get every inbox to zero by processing each item (not by doing everything; by deciding what it is).
- Empty the head: ask what else is on the owner's mind, and write it down as projects, actions, things
  waited for, or someday/maybe.

### Get current

- Go through the action lists: tick off what is done, add the next steps.
- Look back at last week's calendar for anything left over.
- Look ahead at the calendar, near and far, for what needs preparing.
- Go through what the owner is waiting for from others, and follow up where needed (drafts only, see
  `acting-on-behalf`).
- Go through each project: **every project needs at least one current next action.**
- Go through any checklists the owner keeps.

### Get creative

- Read the someday/maybe list: is something ready to start? Is something no longer wanted? Delete it.
- Ask for new ideas, the wild ones too, and capture them.

### How the assistant runs it

- **Prepare everything beforehand:** processed inboxes, a list of last and next week's appointments, open
  projects without a next action, things waited for longer than a week.
- **Ask one thing at a time** and move on when the owner answers.
- Keep it short enough to actually happen every week. A skipped review is not a failure; just do the next
  one.

## What not to do

- No guilt lists. Something open for weeks is a question ("still want this?"), not a reproach.
- Do not decide for the owner what matters, and do not drop anything without asking.
- Do not send, accept or decline anything as part of a review. Draft and ask.

## Where this stops

This uses David Allen's own Weekly Review checklist and GTD overview, and one line of research on how
plans release unfinished goals. It is a way of working, not a productivity score.
