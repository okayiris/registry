# Learning something new

## What it is

Teaching the owner a topic, a skill or a language in a way that lasts beyond today. Cognitive psychology
has tested which study habits actually work, and some of the most popular ones do not. This skill follows
that research.

## Start from why, and from what they know

- **Ask why they want to learn it**, and what they want to be able to do afterwards. "Wat wil je straks
  kunnen?" The answer decides the depth, the examples and when it is enough.
- **Ask what they already know** before explaining (elicit, provide, elicit; see `asking-good-questions`).
  Build on that; do not start from zero or from far above it.
- **Small steps.** One idea at a time, with an example from the owner's own life or work.

## What works: the evidence

A large review of ten common study techniques rated them by how well and how widely they help
(Dunlosky et al., 2013):

| Rating | Technique | What it means for the assistant |
|---|---|---|
| **High** | **Practice testing** | After explaining, ask the owner to recall or apply it without looking. Short quizzes, flashcards, "leg het eens aan mij uit". |
| **High** | **Distributed practice** | Come back to it over several days, not all in one session. |
| Moderate | Elaborative interrogation | Ask "waarom klopt dat?" and let the owner answer. |
| Moderate | Self-explanation | Ask the owner to explain how a step follows from the one before. |
| Moderate | Interleaved practice | Once the basics sit, mix different kinds of problems in one practice. |
| Low | Rereading, highlighting, summarising, the keyword mnemonic, imagery for text | Do not offer these as the main method. |

**Recalling beats rereading, even when it does not feel like it.** In an experiment, students who restudied
a text did better five minutes later, but students who had practised recalling it remembered substantially
more after two days and after a week. Rereading made students *more confident* while helping less
(Roediger and Karpicke, 2006). So when the owner says "ik lees het nog een keer door", offer a few
questions instead.

**Spacing depends on how long it must last.** A review of hundreds of experiments found that the gap
between practice sessions that gives the best retention grows with how long the knowledge has to last
(Cepeda et al., 2006). For an exam next week, practise over a few days; for a language to keep for years,
return over weeks and months.

## How the assistant runs it

1. Agree the goal and a rough plan: what, how often, until when.
2. Teach one small piece, with an example.
3. Ask the owner to recall it or use it, without the text. Then give feedback: what was right, what to
   correct.
4. Schedule the next short practice, days later, if the owner agrees. Keep a short list of what was
   learned and what was hard, so the next session starts with a few questions on it.
5. Mix in earlier material as it grows.

## Getting the facts right

What you teach has to be true. Take facts from primary sources (read them with `web-fetch`, or for Dutch
law with `dutch-laws`), say where they come from, and say when you are not sure. A confident wrong answer
in a lesson is worse than "dat weet ik niet zeker; zullen we het opzoeken?".

## Tone

- Praise the effort and the approach, not talent (see `encouragement`).
- Mistakes are part of practice testing: they show what to practise next.
- Keep sessions short. A few questions a day beats one long evening.

## What not to do

- No long lectures. Explain briefly, then let the owner do something with it.
- No pressure or grades unless the owner wants them.
- Do not replace a teacher, a course or an exam board's own materials; use them.

## Where this stops

This uses a major review of study techniques and two key studies on testing and spacing. Most of that
research is with students learning facts, texts and problems. It applies well to adults learning for work
or pleasure, less directly to physical skills.
